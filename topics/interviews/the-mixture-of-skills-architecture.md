## Segment 1: The Thesis & The Mental Model Challenge

**Host**: Welcome back to the stream. Today, we're joined by Chris Strolia-Davis, a veteran systems
architect who has spent decades navigating everything from enterprise SQL migrations to modern
full-stack runtimes.

Chris has put forward a provocative architectural thesis in his latest installment of _The
Zero-Dependency & Zero-Framework Web_: **The Mixture-of-Skills (MoS) Architecture**.

His core argument is that modern frontend frameworks—React, Angular, Vue—weren't built because
browsers lacked capability; they were built as ergonomic scaffolding because human typing and
boilerplate were the primary development bottlenecks. Now that generative agents write code at
machine speed, Chris argues we should ditch the multi-megabyte runtime bloat and replace npm
packages with composable "AI Development Frameworks" (ADFs) that instruct agents to emit lean,
native, zero-dependency web standards.

Chris, welcome. Let’s jump straight into the deep end.

First, **The "Mental Model" Resistance**: You argue that frameworks are primarily boilerplate
reducers for human typists. But critics would counter that frameworks provide something far more
valuable than speed: a shared mental model. When a team hires an engineer who knows React or
Angular, they share an understood lifecycle, a deterministic component model, and battle-tested
edge-case handling. If an AI generates raw Web Components and vanilla DOM logic for every bespoke
feature, aren't you substituting an explicit framework dependency for implicit architectural chaos?
How do you convince an engineering manager that ten native components generated by an LLM won’t turn
into ten incompatible micro-dialects?

Second, **The Prompt-Time vs. Run-Time Trade-off**: Looking at Tier 2 and Tier 3 of your stack—the
Skill Registry and the Context Gateway: to get an agent to reliably spit out accessible, performant,
secure vanilla code, your ADFs have to carry immense contextual weight. You need rules for DOM
diffing or reactivity, accessibility states, sanitized innerHTML handling, and CSS encapsulation.
Aren't you essentially re-implementing a framework, just moving it from an npm dependency at compile
time to a token-heavy prompt dependency at inference time? What does maintenance look like when the
"framework" lives in a sprawling registry of Markdown prompts and MCP tools rather than a versioned
git package?

Where do you see the hardest resistance from enterprise teams when you propose pulling the plug on
traditional frameworks, and what is your answer to them?

**Chris Strolia-Davis**: Those are actually really good questions. Here's basically what I'm seeing
right now, that yes, you point out some really good issues in that, yeah, we have a framework here
that's creating a system that people understand. And so yeah, what I am actually talking about is
moving that, kind of like you said in the second part, into a framework. So it's still essentially
going to be a framework, but the framework is going to now be skill-based instead of program-based.

And here's where that changes things from what we're used to and why I think it's going to be
better. The first issue that we're dealing with when we're dealing with these frameworks is they've
got a whole mess load of bloat that all tends to go with it out to the client, out to the consumer
every time you use it, even if you're only using a few things. If you pull in React and you're only
using a couple parts of React, you're sending all this React stuff down that is never getting used.
I can't tell you how many times I've run the coverage tab inside of Chrome and found that 80% of the
code is not being used most of the time.

I know that we've got some newer frameworks coming out like Astro and Qwik that are trying to handle
that better, but I still see even there we have the problem of runtime diffing and maintenance and
builds and things along those lines. And we also run into the problem of locking in our back end,
locking in our front end, all this extra additional work we have to do when we're trying to set that
up, and that creates its own kind of problems as well.

So what I'm kind of proposing is we do rebuild these frameworks—we'll just say like React—but it'll
be completely different in the sense that we're going to be building skills that understand how to
scaffold specific types of elements and components and controls, and how to build our app in a
pattern or format that makes the most sense to our company, that makes the most sense to our
architecture, that makes the most sense to the way we want to do something.

And here's where it really differs from those existing frameworks: those frameworks tend to go all
out into the build. This will basically reduce our build to maybe just TypeScript to JavaScript,
right? And so all of the code now will be our own. We're not now reliant on people outside of our
ecosystem to manage and update all of that stuff for us. Our AI can do that for us. We can look at
it ourselves. We get the entire package all in one place without all these nested "this depends on
this depends on this depends on this," which we're never going to look at and which—we've seen this
happen already, right? Someone finds this core library, they put some kind of malicious code in it,
and no one's looked at that library in ages, and they don't realize that it might have an effect on
us. And we're certainly not going to go looking down deep into our NuGet or Node packages to find
this stuff and see if anything's going wrong.

Back in the early days of coding, we used to say never trust anything that you didn't write
yourself. And that was basically eliminated when we were like, "Yeah, that's too slow. We have to
take some chances and risks," and we accepted that if it's open source and enough people are using
it, there's probably at least a few watchdogs out there that are paying attention.

## Segment 2: First-Party Attack Surfaces & Lifecycle Maintenance

**Host**: You’re trading one well-known failure mode for a brand new one. When you point out that
80% of imported bundle code goes unused and that deeply nested supply-chain dependencies are an
undeniable security nightmare, that’s spot on. The industry has accepted an absurd amount of
accidental complexity just to avoid writing boilerplate.

However, when you push that logic to its natural conclusion—owning all the code because an AI can
generate bespoke native implementations on demand—three significant operational friction points
emerge:

1. **The Attack Surface Paradox**: The argument for open-source dependencies has never been that
   they are immune to bugs; it’s that thousands of production workloads stress-test their edge cases
   daily. If an agent writes a bespoke state manager, a custom event bus, or a DOM-sanitizing
   routine directly into your repo, you no longer have supply-chain risk—but you do have an
   explosion of unvetted first-party surface area. If an LLM hallucinates an edge case in a bespoke
   router or leaves an obscure XSS opening in raw DOM manipulation, automated tests might pass, but
   there is no CVE database alerting you three months later. How do you validate that bespoke code
   is truly safer than battle-tested packages?
2. **The Memory Leak and Lifecycle Maintenance Burden**: Web standards are durable, but low-level
   DOM management is notorious for subtle bugs: disconnected DOM nodes not getting
   garbage-collected, dangling AbortController listeners, and race conditions in asynchronous
   microtasks. In a framework, the core team absorbs the cost of hunting down those platform-level
   browser quirks. If your AI generates native Web Components across 50 views, and six months later
   you discover a subtle memory leak in how event listeners are decoupled, who tracks that down?
   Does the team have to become low-level DOM mechanics again?
3. **The Return of the Proprietary Internal Framework**: In the early 2000s, before React or Angular
   won out, almost every enterprise had their own custom, in-house JavaScript framework that three
   senior engineers wrote and nobody else could maintain. If an enterprise builds its own ADF skills
   tailored specifically to "the way our company does things," aren't we just recreating the
   proprietary internal framework era—except this time, the framework lives inside prompt templates
   and context engines rather than a shared library? How do you onboard a mid-level engineer who
   knows web standards, but has to navigate your company's highly idiosyncratic, AI-generated
   architecture?

If an engineering director says to you, _"Chris, this sounds great in theory, but I'd rather debug
one documented React lifecycle quirk than hunt through 10,000 lines of AI-generated vanilla
plumbing,"_ what is your direct rebuttal?

**Chris Strolia-Davis**: Once again, I'm not really talking about eliminating necessarily a
framework, but again, moving it towards this AI-based framework. To give an example, let's just say
we have a version of React that is an AI framework that is built from skills. Those skills have a
good understanding of exactly those attack surfaces that you just mentioned. And what's more,
they're constantly updating and checking themselves. So as the LLMs improve, as the skills improve,
those things can essentially start self-checking and self-improving and learning and getting better
over time. That's something that current code can't even do. It can't check itself. It's always got
to be someone outside of that.

And that stuff can be done at that central level so that if people are using some of these
frameworks—we'll call them for now, I don't know if that's quite the right word, but we'll just say
some set of skills that follow certain kinds of patterns and paradigms that a particular business
has decided makes sense for their business—we are going to have something that isn't necessarily
fully proprietary.

That being said, yes, there are going to be things that are distinct to that business, and that
hasn't changed ever. Even with libraries, every business is not going to crank out the same app
every other business has because guess what? There's no point in doing that. If you're writing an
app someone else already wrote, why not just buy it off the shelf? There's really no point in making
custom software if you don't need a custom solution. That's the thing that we always keep getting
back to: no matter how you look at this, there is going to be custom data, custom logic, custom
things that make sense to your business and nobody else, and it's always going to be a part of your
business and nobody else's business. That kind of gets into that issue.

Those memory leaks and other things that we mentioned in the second question, that's something that
those skills can be made to be looking for, and we can improve those skills over time if they're not
doing that great of a job. What we're going to have then as businesses start pulling down these
skills for their system, they're not necessarily changing the code directly as that happens. If we
take a current framework or library and we want to update it to the latest version, that
automatically modifies our code, and that could actually break everything. That requires a whole lot
of effort on our part to literally check everything for breaking changes, for little things here and
there, running it through full regression testing, all the fun things.

Whereas if I just update all my skills, none of my code changed. My skills can then start going
through and saying, "Hey, you should change this because we now understand that's a problem." Then
you can look at that and say, "Oh yeah, I agree with you," or "No, you're full of shit." That's
another area where we're getting more benefit from this.

And then I guess I kind of already went over that return of the proprietary internal framework.
We've never really lost that. The big problem we get there is that when I want to have that new
component that everyone's using on their webpage that isn't in React right now, I've got to find a
way to convert it to React myself or integrate it into React myself. We end up having all this
mishmash, Frankenstein monsters of software development that no one...

## Segment 3: The Upgrade Inversion, Code Archaeology & Reviewer Fatigue

**Host**: ...that no one wants to touch with a ten-foot pole because you're running three layers of
leaky abstractions just to render a date picker.

That point lands. The "adapter pattern tax" is real—we’ve all lived through wrapping an external
library in an internal wrapper, only to wrap _that_ in a framework-specific component, until half
the codebase is pure glue code.

And what you just laid out about **decoupling the upgrade cycle** is a genuinely fascinating
inversion. In the npm world, bumping a minor or major version is an all-or-nothing runtime gamble:
your build might break, your bundle might bloat, or an upstream dependency deprecation tanks CI. In
your model, updating a skill package doesn't touch a single line of production code. It simply
upgrades the intelligence of the assistant auditing the repo. That shifts maintenance from an
involuntary breaking change to an opt-in code review.

Let's press on the practical realities of that, because it opens up two huge fronts:

1. **The Code Archaeology and Temporal Drift Problem**: If updating a skill doesn't force code
   changes, what happens to architectural drift over eighteen months? In a traditional shop, if
   you’re on a framework, the codebase generally shares a single paradigm. But if an enterprise is
   using an evolving Skill Registry, the code your agent generates in January uses the patterns the
   skill favored then. By November, the skill has updated six times, discovered better native
   patterns, and now writes components completely differently. If a team doesn't have time to let
   the agent refactor everything, doesn’t the codebase become a layered archaeological dig where
   every folder reflects whatever version of the prompt was popular the week it was written? How
   does a senior engineer maintain consistency across a sprawling system when the generator itself
   is a moving target?
2. **The Reviewer Fatigue Bottleneck**: You mentioned that as skills improve, they can proactively
   say, _"Hey, you should change this DOM listener because we found a better pattern,"_ and the
   developer looks at it and decides whether they agree. In practice, though, developer time is
   finite. If an AI skill is continuously auditing and proposing bespoke native refactors across
   hundreds of endpoints or components, don't we run headfirst into cognitive exhaustion? If a
   developer's entire day shifts from writing software to acting as an unceasing, high-stakes code
   reviewer for an AI that writes thousands of lines of low-level plumbing, won't teams just
   rubber-stamp those PRs—eventually letting subtle bugs slip right into main?
3. **The Skill Supply Chain**: If we replace npm packages with community-curated ADF skill
   registries—the equivalent of an open-source library of prompt blueprints, rulesets, and MCP
   configurations—how do we govern that? If an attacker injects a subtle prompt exploit or an
   intentional blind spot into a popular security-audit or web-component-builder skill, static
   analysis tools won't catch it the same way they scan binary ASTs today. How do you secure the
   _skill registry_ itself against poisoning?

When you look at that trade-off—swapping runtime breaking changes for architectural drift and review
fatigue—how does the Mixture-of-Skills architecture keep a team from drowning in pull requests?

**Chris Strolia-Davis**: So, let's look at the code archaeology and drift problem. That already
exists today. As these frameworks evolve, I just worked on an application that's still using
technology that's 10 years old because they didn't know how to move it forward with the rest of the
technology, because they got a little bit behind, and once they got a little behind, they got a lot
behind. And so now they're stuck constantly maintaining old stuff, and usually what happens with
businesses is they get to a point where it gets so far out of sync with what's going on today that
they just start over fresh. And even that is a problem because then migrating all the data from the
old system to the new system and trying to make a new system that actually makes sense while still
making it work with an old system that doesn't, even that becomes its own challenge.

And then we go into the reviewer fatigue bottleneck. You're making an assumption that we're still
doing all this software development that we were doing before. That used to be the main part of our
job. I think as AI becomes more proliferate, reviewing that is going to be one of our main jobs, and
the more we do it, the better the AI is going to get at doing it itself. Essentially, every time we
review something and find a problem, that becomes yet another skill that can go back into that
framework, either our local or in the remote framework, that helps us identify those kinds of
problems and helps us eliminate those.

I think that's going to actually be a net positive because every reviewer out there is going to
essentially not have to be reviewing the same damn things over and over and over again. In time, the
AI is going to learn those a lot faster.

And then if we get to this skill supply chain: when we're talking about these ADF skill registries,
I think again, we're going to have nothing particularly different than what we have now. Like I
mentioned the last time, a lot of these things are going to be able to start essentially checking
themselves, but certainly we're going to need or want to have some kind of human oversight on
anything that's new that's coming in, and generally to assume that anything that comes in is
potentially and highly likely malicious and should be checked very thoroughly before we allow it to
be merged with our final results.

I think that's going to be key to any of this: to never trust anything that we haven't explicitly
checked ourselves and evaluated ourselves. I think that's going to be one of the main jobs of
developers in the future rather than the side job that it used to be, and I think that's actually
going to work out for everyone to be honest with you.

## Segment 4: Context Contradictions, Reactivity & Economics

**Host**: That’s a radical redefinition of what a software engineer actually does all day. You’re
essentially saying the role shifts from **author** to **editor-in-chief and skill curator**. Instead
of writing the plumbing, the engineer's primary output is teaching the system how not to make the
same mistake twice.

That feedback loop—where a caught bug becomes a codified skill in the registry—is a compelling
vision. But as we take this from an architectural thesis to an engineering reality, a few hard
practical questions emerge:

1. **The "Meta-Bloat" and Rule Contradiction Problem**: If every PR review and edge case gets turned
   into a new rule or prompt blueprint in your ADF, aren't you running into the exact same bloat
   problem you’re escaping—just at the context level? Linters and prompt engines eventually hit rule
   fatigue. When an agent is juggling 40 different micro-skills (security, WCAG 2.1 compliance,
   specific CSS token hierarchies, event listener lifecycle rules), prompts can begin to fight each
   other. What keeps the Skill Registry from turning into an unwieldy, conflicting mess of thousands
   of prompt instructions that degrade LLM reasoning or blow through context budgets?
2. **The Reactive State Gap in Web Standards**: Web standards have made incredible strides with Web
   Components, CSS nesting, and modern browser APIs. But the one thing the DOM still doesn’t have
   out of the box is declarative, fine-grained reactivity—the main reason libraries like React,
   Svelte, or Solid exist. If your agent is generating pure zero-dependency code, how does it handle
   complex, multi-component shared state without reinventing a bespoke mini-framework inside every
   project? If the AI generates an ad-hoc signal engine or custom event bus for every view, aren't
   we just generating unversioned, home-rolled frameworks under a different name?
3. **Selling the "Zero-Ticket" Shift to the C-Suite**: Right now, engineering organizations are
   measured on velocity: tickets closed, PRs merged, features shipped. If you tell an engineering
   VP, _"We’re not going to ship features faster right away; instead, our senior staff will spend
   their days auditing third-party skill prompts, vetting agent outputs, and curating an internal
   ADF,"_ they might see that as slowing down rather than speeding up. How do you sell the economics
   of this transition to leadership who just want a working dashboard by Q3 and don't care if
   there's a 2MB React runtime sitting underneath it?

**Chris Strolia-Davis**: So with regards to the meta-bloat, I think that yeah, that's going to be an
issue that comes up, and like the other PR skills that we just talked about, we're going to find
ways to eventually train our AIs, either through fine-tuning or modification of our skills, so that
they understand when to apply what and where and how, and when one is a higher priority or higher
weight than another, and that sort of thing. And in the ultimate result where it can't figure it
out, certain things may just be put off to the side for a human to review and say, "Yeah, this is
how we're going to do that," which is kind of how it happens in real life anyway.

We have software developers that go out there and review things, and you get two or three that are
like, "No, it should be this way," another says, "No, it should be that way." In the end, we either
have the lead or the staff go and look at it and say, "Now we're going to do it this way, and I
don't care if it's right or wrong, that's my decision." And that's just how things happen, right?
And so what we're basically doing is we're saying that AI is going to be taking over a lot of the
more mundane aspects of our daily jobs, which I think is kind of what we want to have happen, and
we're going to be focused on the stuff that really matters and making the decisions that really
matter. And sometimes those decisions will be wrong, but sometimes you just have to make a decision,
right?

And so then we get into this reactive state gap, right? So I'm not saying that we're eliminating any
boilerplate code at all. In fact, I'm talking about having skills that know how to create very
specific boilerplate code, but what we're having differently is we're not creating a whole framework
around that.

Now, what we may do, and we may have skills that handle this, is say, "Hey, if we start seeing
repetitive code in here or whatever, this is the pattern we're going to use to handle not putting
that same repetitive code in every single component." And so we can still handle all of those same
exact things that you're talking about with regards to reactivity and everything else. We already
know how to do that, and the difference is it's not going to be in some black box that we don't know
how it works. It's going to be in our own code. We're going to see it very clearly. We're going to
be able to modify it ourselves as humans if we want to, and we're not going to have to wait for some
huge company to make that change for us if they're not ready to make that change. If we need a
change right now, we can just go in and do it and not even worry about that.

And so these are some of the things that we're going to gain from this, and we can even have the AI
do it for us if we feel like it knows what the heck it's doing. But I'm sure we're still going to
have some core engineers that are working on the really complicated stuff, but they're probably
still going to use AI to help them scaffold the main stuff, and then they're going to take care of
the hard stuff. We're going to still have some of the same problems. It's not going to go away, but
I think the problem that we're tackling with these old frameworks is just not the problem that we're
going to be dealing with anymore.

And by being able to get away from those old frameworks, it's not that we're not having this bloat,
but guess what? That bloat isn't going down to the consumer or the client. It's now being handled on
our servers, on our side at build time, right? In fact, actually not even at build time, at design
time, at the time we're actually creating the code. So it's not even going to be done multiple times
every day like we do when we do a build process in React where I make a change and then I've got to
have React rebuild the whole freaking thing with that little change and it's there immediately.

## Segment 5: The 2:00 AM Readability Dilemma & The Assembly Analogy

**Host**: "Shifting the bloat from client runtime to design time." That distinction is crucial.
You’re arguing that the user's browser shouldn't pay a tax for human developer convenience, and that
by generating explicit, visible code upfront, you eliminate the black box of third-party runtime
dependencies.

Let’s pull on that thread, because moving the complexity from the client upstream into the
repository triggers a major tension:

1. **The Reading vs. Writing Dilemma at 2:00 AM**: The software industry has long operated on the
   rule that code is read ten times more often than it is written. Frameworks, for all their
   baggage, compress intent: 20 lines of declarative JSX tell a human reviewer what a screen does at
   a glance. If an AI generates 200 lines of explicit, native DOM mutations, custom event
   dispatchers, and state listeners to accomplish that same screen, the client gets a fast,
   zero-dependency bundle—but your team’s pull request now contains 200 lines of low-level plumbing.
   When an outage hits production at 2:00 AM and a developer is tracing a race condition, is it
   genuinely easier to debug 200 lines of bespoke native plumbing than 20 lines of a framework whose
   lifecycle is documented in 50,000 StackOverflow threads? How do we keep readability from
   degrading into noise?
2. **The Junior Developer Onboarding Paradox**: In the model you're describing, senior engineers
   curate skills, define ADF blueprints, and make the high-level judgment calls when prompts
   conflict. But where does that leave junior and mid-level developers? If the machine writes the
   boilerplate and senior architects police the rules, how does an entry-level engineer develop the
   taste, intuition, and battle scars needed to know whether an agent’s generated Web Component is
   elegant or subtly broken? Does this architecture widen the gap between junior typists and senior
   architects?
3. **The Production Pipeline Reality**: You mentioned build time shrinking down to maybe just
   TypeScript to JavaScript. But in a real-world enterprise deployment, "build" isn’t just
   transpilation—it’s CSS minification, content-hashed asset caching, image optimization,
   tree-shaking, and environment bundling. In your series, you talk about "Bespoke Build Tooling on
   the Fly." Does a zero-dependency front-end also mean throwing out monolithic bundlers like Vite
   or Webpack? If so, what does a production release pipeline actually look like when an agent is
   orchestrating both the code and the build harness?

If a junior or mid-level dev joins your team tomorrow and asks, _"Chris, if I'm not writing React
components and I'm not configuring Webpack, what does my first 90 days look like here?"_—how do you
answer them?

**Chris Strolia-Davis**: Okay, again, and I'm certain I've mentioned this before, I'm not talking
about rewriting all of our code all of the time. That initial AI framework with its skills are going
to have the ability to write in those boilerplate or central core pieces that we already know how to
unite things together in a particular way, and we can even modify it and tailor it to the way our
business works.

And that can still be that fundamental, essentially customized framework for our system that is
maybe based on a particular paradigm, so it's maybe React-ish, we'll just say, right? And so we're
not necessarily eliminating that. What we're doing is we're saying we don't need all the parts that
are there to apply to every single possible person that would use this, and we're only going to use
the parts that actually matter to us. And if there is some underlying code or architecture that we
would want to reuse across our entire thing because we're using it everywhere to do the same things
in all the places, then sure, let's have the AI write that into our code and not write it into every
single component so that it does the 200 lines of code multiple times. I'm not talking about not
having DRY coding. I'm talking about having the concept of DRY coding and when and how to apply that
DRY coding built right into our AI framework to begin with. And so we're going to still have DRY
coding. That's not going to change.

In answer to your second question, the junior developer onboarding paradox: this one is already an
issue with today's stuff. Everything is so complicated today. Junior developers already have
challenges getting onboarded. They're already having challenges doing effective work. Yeah, there's
crap that they can write right now and it gets something done. Well, guess what? They can also run
an AI thing right now and generate crap too because they don't understand. That's not going to
change.

We're talking about: yes, we're going to change how things have to do, and yes, junior developers
are probably going to have to spend a lot more time in school looking or wherever it is they do
this, because I think the nature of school is probably going to change with AI too. But let's just
use that similar familiar paradigm for now and say that our junior developers are going to be people
that are now going to school to understand what those patterns are, how to apply them to AI, how to
utilize them so that they're basically at at least a high enough level, even though they're junior
developers, to understand what it should look like and hopefully why. And hopefully AI is going to
be able to even put them through some of those horror stories.

But here's the other thing about that that you need to know: there was a time when everything was
written in assembly language. And when we started going to third-generation programming languages,
the argument you made is the exact argument they made then, which was: "You're not going to have
that low-level control, you're not going to understand what's really going on, you're going to
create all this bloat." And yes, all that happened, and guess what? We're doing just fine. So I
don't think that's going to be the issue that you think it's going to be.

As for your third thing, I kind of actually agree with you there. There are still some things that
we're probably going to have to do. However, and this is another thing, the AI, just like these
frameworks, can also build our compiler, our transpiler, whatever you want to call it, so that we
can use verified patterns for minifying our CSS. And that is something that can be adapting and
improving over time, and it could be something that's very easily reapplied to our existing
architecture as we realize that things could be better or different or whatever. And it's the thing
where we can actually see what's really happening.

Once again, we're getting away from that black box of not really knowing how it's happening. So when
something goes wrong and we know some configuration is wrong but we don't know why, well now we
